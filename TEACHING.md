# Teaching principles

These principles apply to every Econ Lab model.

## Lead with the question

Start with a puzzle the model can answer, never the setup. Explain why each modeling step is useful before displaying symbols. The goal is intuition, not memorization of a derivation.

## Five parts, always in this order

1. **Why this step:** one sentence explaining what we need to figure out and why.
2. **Plain English:** short, concrete reasoning, with a clearly marked example when useful.
3. **The math:** symbolic relationships, followed by live values using the current state.
4. **On the chart:** what changed and what to notice.
5. **Show the math:** an optional expander with the extra algebra.

Introduce every quantity in words before its symbol. Keep names consistent. In the Holmström–Tirole lesson the main language is **honesty premium**, **skin in the game**, and **temptation per borrowed dollar**. Mention the textbook term for the maximum incentive-compatible repayment only once; do not reorganize the explanation around it.

Every stated assumption gets a “What if this weren't true?” explanation with a visual. Distinguish conceptual counterfactuals from solved extensions. Do not apply a closed-form solution outside the assumptions used to derive it.

Use small **Careful:** notes for misconceptions. End with **So what?**: answer the opening puzzle, connect carefully to evidence, and give one sentence the reader can take away. Captions should be short and concrete. Translate jargon next to its first use.

## Accuracy and attribution

- State that this is the simplified Holmström–Tirole model taught in GU4710, not the full 1997 paper.
- Cite lecture equations and claims with the deck and **printed** slide number. Keep PDF page positions as a separate lookup.
- Mark algebraic rearrangements “From Slides …”. Do not imply that the slides use our invented terminology or diagrams.
- Label original real-world analogies **For example**. List all original additions in the model's sources.
- The words “skin in the game” are in the slides. “Honesty premium,” “temptation per borrowed dollar,” the meter/per-dollar framing, and R = 1.6 / B = 1.0 are our teaching additions.
- Hurst–Lusardi: a mostly flat wealth–entry relationship with a rise at the top of the distribution. Their findings do not establish a general borrowing-constraint explanation. Our redraw is qualitative, with no invented probability estimates.
- Chaney–Sraer–Thesmar: attribute the 1.8-percentage-point estimate to the authors and specify accumulated investment over 2000–2006 relative to initial assets. Describe consistency with a collateral channel, not proof of this exact model or a universally accepted causal magnitude.
- Shirking destroys the entrepreneur's invested capital as well as the bank's loan. Limited liability means no additional recovery after the project fails, not that her initial contribution is immune to loss.
- Equality follows the lecture's weak incentive constraint: the entrepreneur behaves when indifferent. At B = R and no loan, she is indifferent; at any positive loan she prefers shirking.
- With B ≤ R − 1, the incentive constraint does not yield a finite borrowing limit. This does not produce the finite optimum of the distinct decreasing-returns model in Slides 1.
- Comparisons must use consistent units. Conceptual diagrams and schematics must say that they are not quantitative estimates.

## Predictions and access

Ask for a prediction before revealing a result. Explain why in one sentence. Use stable, disclosed starting values so the question stays meaningful regardless of the playground state. Session scores must not be persisted.

Diagrams need labels and captions, keyboard/touch alternatives, and non-color cues. Motion should connect states, not decorate the page. Respect reduced motion. Optional mathematics must remain readable without animation.
